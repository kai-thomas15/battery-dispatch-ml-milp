Battery Dispatch

A grid battery earns money by charging when power is cheap and selling when it is expensive. I built a dispatch optimiser that plans those trades each day on the German day-ahead market, with a machine-learning price forecast as its input.

The idea in 30 seconds

Prices change every hour

On sunny days, solar panels flood the grid at midday and power gets cheap, sometimes below zero. In the evening the sun is gone, people are home and power gets expensive.

The battery shifts energy in time

It charges when power is cheap and sells it back when it is expensive. The price difference, minus energy losses and wear, is the profit.

The catch: the plan is made before the prices are known

By noon each day, the battery's plan for all of tomorrow must be handed in to the power exchange. Tomorrow's prices are only published afterwards. So the plan has to be based on a prediction, and the better the prediction, the more the battery earns.

How sensitive is it to costs?

Two cost assumptions drive the result: battery wear (the base case charges €10 per MWh discharged) and trading fees (none in the base case). Higher costs cut profit and make the optimiser cycle less, but the edge over the simple rule stays between €3,400 and €3,900 in every setting.

How it works

For the technical reader. The optimiser is the core; the forecast is the input it plans on. Each day is tested using only data that was available before that day's auction.

  1. Forecast

    A machine-learning model (gradient boosting) predicts tomorrow's 24 hourly prices from recent prices and the calendar. It learns from 2023 onward and is retrained every month on past data only.

  2. Plan

    An optimiser (a MILP, which finds the best schedule within hard limits) decides when to charge and sell, respecting the battery's power, storage, energy losses and wear.

  3. Compare

    The plan is paid at the real prices and compared with a simple rule (assume tomorrow repeats today) and perfect hindsight (knowing the real prices in advance, the ceiling no forecast can beat).

A basic model, and what it is missing

The gap to the ceiling is the value of a better forecast. I tested which inputs close it:

What I tested

  • Two more years of price history: the biggest clean gain, from 84% to 91% of the ceiling
  • The grid operators' wind and solar forecasts: up to 95%, but they are published after the auction
  • Public weather forecasts, the demand forecast, holidays and neighbouring prices: a smaller forecast error, but no extra profit yet

Would help, but I have no access

  • Commercial wind and solar forecasts available before the auction
  • Gas and CO₂ prices, which set the cost of the plants that fill the evening peak (licensed market data)
  • Intraday and balancing market data, where batteries earn much of their money (licensed)
  • A real battery's operating data, limits and contracts
